    About the data

    The projections are the IPCC's. Sea levels come from the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6): its medium-confidence projections of total sea-level change, including the rise and fall of the land itself, on a one-degree grid, every ten years from 2020 to 2150, relative to the 1995–2014 average. "Low end" and "High end" are the 17th and 83rd percentiles, the IPCC's likely range. The NASA Sea Level Change Team hosts them in the IPCC AR6 Sea Level Projection Tool.

    The flooding is our calculation. We compare each projected sea level with the elevation of the ground, and let the water in only where it can flow from today's ocean: low land behind higher ground stays dry until the sea tops it. Land already below the projected sea in 2020 is hatched: dikes and pumps keep it dry today. The elevation is Tileflow Terrain, which uses national lidar terrain models in Europe, North America, Japan and Oceania; on other coasts, where it carries a surface model with trees and buildings, we use DeltaDTM, a bare-earth coastal terrain model corrected with satellite lidar, at about 300 m. The population is GHS-POP 2025 from the European Commission's Joint Research Centre.

    What it leaves out. Mean sea level only: no tides, storm surges or waves, which reach higher. No defences beyond those visible in the elevation data. Elevation models have errors of decimetres to metres on low coasts. Read this as an exploration of the projections, not as a flood-risk assessment for any property.
